## READ THIS FIRST — WHAT THIS ROUND IS ACTUALLY TESTING

The technical round is done. An **Executive Director of Support Services** owns the corporate functions — IT, HR, finance, admin. He is not going to test your code. He has four worries, and every answer you give should quietly close one of them:

3. **Can he work with us?** Non-technical stakeholders, other government entities, vendors, protocol, patience.
4. **Is he sensible about AI?** He needs someone who brings AI in safely — not someone who puts student and family data into a public chatbot.

**The one line to land, at least twice:**

## #26 — "Tell me about a time you failed."

## #33 — "How do you stop AI from giving wrong answers?"

**Four things, in this order. This maps exactly onto the JD's guardrails line, in language he can repeat to his own leadership.**

> "**One** — it answers only from our own documents, not from its general knowledge, and it shows the source paragraph. If it can't find a source, it's built to say 'I don't know.'
>
> **Two** — we check the answer before anyone sees it: is it the right shape, does it contain anything it shouldn't.
>
> **Three** — the AI can only touch what we allow it to touch. It gets its own permissions, like an employee, and only for the systems it needs.
>
> **Four** — anything with real consequences has a person approving it. The AI drafts; a human signs.
>
> And we log everything — so if someone asks six months later why the system said something, we can answer that."

# TIER 4 — LONG SHOTS (Under 30%)

*Mostly technical. He probably won't ask these — but if he does, he's testing whether you can explain something complicated simply, which is itself the skill. **No jargon unless you immediately unpack it.***

## #51 — "What is agentic AI?"
> "A normal AI answers a question. An agent is given a goal and a set of tools, and it decides which tools to use to reach that goal — look up a document, query a database, draft an email. The important part is the boundaries: it only gets the tools we hand it, only the permissions those tools need, and anything with a real consequence goes to a person for approval. It drafts; a human signs."

## #52 — "What is RAG?"
> "Instead of trusting the model's memory, we hand it the relevant pages at the moment of the question. We break our documents into small pieces, index them by meaning rather than keyword, and when someone asks something we fetch the handful of pieces that actually answer it and give them to the model with the question. The model's job becomes reading and summarising rather than remembering — and remembering is where these systems go wrong."

## #53 — "Explain CI/CD to someone non-technical."
> "Every time someone changes the code, a machine automatically checks it, tests it, and packages it — and releasing it to production becomes a button rather than a person copying files on a Thursday evening. It sounds bureaucratic and it's the opposite: it's what lets you move quickly without being frightened. And it gives you an audit trail for free — every production change has a name, a date and a reason attached."

## #54 — "Why containers and Kubernetes?"
> "A container packages an application with everything it needs to run, so it behaves the same on my laptop as it does in production — which removes a whole category of 'it worked in testing' failures. Kubernetes is what runs a lot of those containers: it restarts them when they fail, scales them up when demand rises, and lets us deploy without downtime. Azure's managed version, AKS, means we don't run the machinery ourselves."

## #55 — "Microservices or a monolith?"
> "It depends on the organisation more than the technology. Microservices let teams deploy independently — but if you're a small team, they mostly buy you complexity you have to maintain. For an organisation this size I'd start with a well-structured single application and split out only the pieces that genuinely need to scale or release separately. Splitting too early is the more common mistake."

## #56 — "How do you secure an application?"
> "Layers. Validate everything coming in, and never trust the client. Proper identity — Azure AD with single sign-on rather than passwords held in the application. Authorisation checked on the server for every request, not just hidden in the interface. Secrets in Key Vault, never in code. Encryption in transit and at rest. And automated scanning of dependencies in the pipeline, because most breaches come through a library nobody updated rather than clever code."

## #57 — "What is Microsoft Fabric?"
> "Microsoft's attempt to put the whole data platform in one place — storage, pipelines, warehousing, real-time and Power BI on a single foundation called OneLake, so the data is stored once and every tool reads the same copy. The advantage for an organisation like this is fewer moving parts and one governance model, rather than stitching five services together."

## #58 — "Why Power BI rather than building dashboards ourselves?"
> "Because the value isn't the chart, it's who can make one. If reporting only comes from me, I'm a bottleneck forever. Power BI lets the business build its own reports on a governed dataset that I maintain — so I look after the numbers being right, and they look after the questions being asked. That's what the job description means by self-service analytics enablement."

## #59 — "How would you approach redesigning our intranet?"
> "I'd start by finding out what people actually use it for — usually it's four things, and the rest of the site is noise nobody visits. A beautiful intranet with old content is worse than a plain one that's current."

## #60 — "How would you measure whether the digital platform is succeeding?"
> "Three kinds of number. **Is it working** — uptime, response times, incidents. **Is it being used** — active users, whether people come back, which features are actually touched. **Is it saving anything** — hours removed from a manual process, time to complete a request, backlog cleared. The third is the one leadership cares about, and it's the one people forget to measure at the start, when it's the only time you can get a baseline."
